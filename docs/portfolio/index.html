Closing the Deictic Loop

Bidirectional deictic grounding for gesture-conditioned vision-language-action navigation on a quadruped

Pulitha Gunawardana, Agra Navaratne, Rivikula Fernando

Supervised by Dr. Rohan Munasinghe, Dr. Sampath Perera, Dr. Vigneshwaran Subbaraju, Dr. Darshana Rathnayake and Dr. Dulanga Weerakoon
Department of Electronic and Telecommunication Engineering, University of Moratuwa, Sri Lanka

Pointing is the quickest way to say “that one.” A robot running a vision-language-action model answers seconds later. By then the gesture is over, and if the robot has turned in the meantime, a remembered direction is off by exactly that turn. We anchor the pointing ray in the world frame the moment it is seen, let NaVILA pick the target from the live camera view, and have a Unitree Go2 turn, ask, and take a correction before it walks.

    The problem

    A pointing gesture lasts about one to two seconds, and the direction it gives only makes sense relative to where the robot was when it saw it.

    VLA models are slow on edge hardware. While one is still running, the robot may keep turning. If the pointing ray is stored in the robot’s own body frame, it turns with the robot, so by the time the model uses it the ray is wrong by exactly the rotation Δθ. That is a geometric identity, not sensor noise: it can be derived before any experiment and removed by design.

    Small angles matter at room scale. LEGS-POMDP reports an angular error of 14.4°, which is about 1.3 m off at 5 m.

    The gap. No existing system puts these together: pointing input, VLA-driven locomotion on a quadruped, and a channel for the robot to show and check what it understood. We compare directly against the closest prior work, including GIVE and LEGS-POMDP.

    Contributions

    • A provable error model

      An un-anchored pointing ray is off by exactly |Δθ|, the rotation made during inference. The claim holds before any data is collected.

    • Gesture memory buffer

      A fast path that anchors each pointing ray in the world frame through TF2 at capture time, then re-projects it into the robot’s current frame when the VLA needs it.

    • Bidirectional grounding

      A four-state protocol (Perceived, Interpreted, Committed, Executing) in which the robot turns to show its pick, asks, and accepts a confirmation or a correction.

    • Gesture-grounding metrics

      Deictic heading error, instruction capture rate and target selection accuracy, plus how each degrades as injected latency grows from 1 to 5 s, benchmarked in HuNavSim.

    System

    Two paths run side by side. A fast path catches the gesture and stores it in a form that survives the robot’s motion. A slow path runs the VLA on the current view. The grounding manager sits between the VLA and the legs, so nothing moves until the user agrees.

    Speech
    Speech recognition and an instruction parser turn the spoken command into ℓ: the verb plus the referent.
    (a) Gesture perception
    MediaPipe Pose Landmarker tracks the user. Four pointing-ray hypotheses come from the arm keypoints, and a scale-free hold detector decides when a point has been held.
    (b) Gesture memory buffer
    Keeps the pointing frame f* and the ray, anchored with the camera pose at capture time t0. On use, it re-projects the ray into the robot’s current frame.
    Deictic prompt
    Draws the re-projected ray rt onto the live frame It, so the VLA sees where the user pointed.
    (c) NaVILA
    The vision-language-action model reads the prompted frame with ℓ and outputs a mid-level action such as a turn or a short walk.
    (d) Grounding manager and (e) locomotion
    Runs the four-state protocol and routes the user’s reply. Approved actions go to the CHAMP controller, which drives the Unitree Go2.

    Walkthrough

    One instruction from start to finish, including a correction.

    1. 1

      Point and speak

      You point at a box and say “go to that box.” Speech carries the verb; the gesture resolves “that.”

    2. 2

      Perceived

      The hold detector fires after about 1–2 s. The pointing frame f* is kept and the ray is anchored in the world frame.

    3. 3

      Interpreted

      NaVILA reads the live camera frame with the re-projected ray rt drawn in, and picks the box it thinks you meant.

    4. 4

      Committed

      The robot turns to face its pick, which already shows you what it understood, then asks “Is this the direction?”

    5. 5

      Corrected

      “No, turn more right.” The robot applies a heading correction Δψ, turns, and asks again.

    6. 6

      Executing

      “Yes.” Only now does the robot walk to the box, and it stops in front of it.

    Grounding loop

    The robot never walks on a guess. It first turns to face what it thinks you meant, which you can read at a glance, then asks. Your reply decides what happens next.

    You sayThe robot
    “Yes.”Moves to Executing: walks to the target and stops in front of it.
    “No, turn more right.” or “…more left.”Applies a heading correction Δψ in that direction, turns, and asks again.
    A new instruction, such as “No, the box by the door.”Goes back to Interpreted and grounds again with the new words and the stored ray.
    You point againGoes back to Perceived: a new ray is anchored and the old one is dropped.

    Data collection

    We collect in two places: a lab with the real robot, and a Gazebo simulation with HuNavSim pedestrians, where latency sweeps are repeatable.

    Logged per trial

    • RGB-D frames V and speech audio A
    • Pose keypoints k
    • TF2 tree and odometry
    • Velodyne LiDAR scans
    • Ground-truth target pose

    Varied across trials

    • User-to-target distance
    • Target layout
    • Injected latency τ from 1 to 5 s

    Evaluation

    Deictic heading error
    Angle between the robot’s final heading and the true bearing of the target.
    Instruction capture rate
    Share of pointing instructions the hold detector catches.
    Target selection accuracy
    Share of trials where the robot commits to the right object.
    Latency-conditioned degradation
    Each metric above as injected latency τ rises from 1 to 5 s.

    We compare a body-frame ray against the world-anchored ray, first in HuNavSim, then on the real Go2.

    Results will be added here once the evaluation runs.
